# Research: is the problem real, who has it, and what already exists

Written 24 Sep 2026. Desk research only: **nobody in a facility has been asked yet.** Everything here is from published studies, government documents and product websites (sources at the end). Setting is Australian residential aged care (RACFs) unless it says otherwise.

It covers:

1. the short answer
2. polypharmacy, and why the cup doesn't fix it
3. the medication round: where time and errors really go
4. charting
5. swallowing and crushing
6. the rules on residents taking their own medicines
7. what already exists
8. where the value is, and where it isn't
9. the original proposal's claims, checked
10. questions only a facility or speech pathologist can answer

---

## 1. The short answer

- The problems are **real**: long medication rounds, missed and late doses, and no proof that a dose was swallowed.
- The cup **does not fix polypharmacy**, and **charting is the weakest claim**, because Australian facilities already chart electronically.
- The cup only works for residents who can **swallow whole tablets with thin water and lift a cup**. About half of residents have dysphagia and about half have dementia, so the cup can't serve the residents who take the most nurse time.
- The strongest fit found so far is **supported self-administration**. Australian rules already let assessed residents take their own medicines, but require locked storage, adherence monitoring and records. The cup and base do exactly those three jobs.
- **Time-critical medicines** (Parkinson's especially) are a second, smaller strong fit.

---

## 2. Polypharmacy: what it is, and what fixes it

Polypharmacy means taking many medicines, usually 5 or more, some of which may no longer be needed or may be inappropriate.

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| 98% of residents have at least one medicine-related problem | PSA, *Medicine Safety: Aged Care* (2020) |
| Over half are exposed to at least one potentially inappropriate medicine | PSA |
| Over 40% take 9 or more medicines | PSA |
| Typical resident in one trial: median 9 regular medicines, about 4 administration times a day | SIMPLER trial |

**What fixes it:** prescribing-side work: medication reviews, deprescribing, regimen simplification, on-site pharmacists. Since 1 July 2024 the government funds **on-site pharmacists** in residential aged care homes (the ACOP measure) for exactly this. The SIMPLER trial showed that simplifying regimens (fewer administration times) is possible and lasts.

**What this means for us:** the cup can't take a medicine away, so it doesn't fix polypharmacy. It can only help with the **workload and errors polypharmacy creates at the bedside**. The pitch must not claim more.

## 3. The medication round

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| One round takes **2.5–4.5 hours**; about **200 s per resident** | Qian et al. 2016 (time-and-motion study, Australian RACF) |
| About **5 minutes per resident per round** across three facilities | SIMPLER trial |
| Time rises with the number of medicine types and tablets | Qian 2016 |
| Memory support units take **an extra minute per resident**; almost **half of tablets and capsules were crushed** there | Qian 2016 |
| **Missed doses** make up about 32–50% of administration errors; in one electronic-records study **73% of residents had at least one missed dose** | omissions study (2025) |
| UK study: **7% of administered doses** had an administration error (2.1–15.9% by facility) | UK long-term care study |
| Among the top medication **complaint** categories: **not receiving medication at the right time** | complaints study |
| Parkinson's (NSW, 23 facilities, 202 residents on Parkinson's medicines = 7.9% of residents, Jan–Sep 2024): **25.6% of 164,252 doses** given more than 30 min before or after the scheduled time; **every** resident had at least one such dose; 1.1% of doses missed | Urwin et al., JAMDA 2025 (Macquarie University) |

**Takeaways:**

- The time in a round goes on preparing, handing over, **waiting for the swallow**, and handling refusals and swallowing problems. It doesn't go on the charting tap.
- A long round means the last resident gets their "8 am" dose hours late. A cup that delivers on schedule, wherever the round is, fixes that for its users.
- Parkinson's medicines need to be within 30 minutes, and late doses bring back tremor and rigidity and raise fall and aspiration risk. That's a sharp, measurable problem.
- **Caveat on the Parkinson's figure:** it compares the **scheduled clock time in the electronic chart** with the time the dose was actually recorded. The abstract doesn't give reasons or say whether meals were considered. Some deviations may be reasonable: a resident asleep or out, or a dose refused and offered again. Treat 25.6% as the upper bound on harmful timing errors. The full paper may break it down; read it before quoting the figure in the proposal.

**Meal-linked medicines.** Some orders are tied to meals, not the clock ("with food", "30 minutes before breakfast"). The cup can't know when a meal ends, but in a facility this matters less than it seems:

- Meals are served at fixed times, and the electronic chart already turns meal-linked orders into **clock times** (for example "with breakfast" becomes 08:00). The base would use the same clock times.
- **"Before food"** orders (thyroid tablets, some stomach-acid tablets) are timed from a known meal start, so a clock time works.
- **"With or after food"** orders usually have a wide window, typically the meal or shortly after.
- Parkinson's medicines are generally scheduled at **fixed clock times**, often spaced through the day and sometimes kept away from high-protein meals. They're a good fit.
- If a resident's meals really do vary (at home, for example), the fallback is a **"meal finished" button** on the base for the resident or a carer. Or keep those medicines off the cup.

## 4. Charting

- Australia is moving residential aged care to the **electronic National Residential Medication Chart (eNRMC)**. Vendors have run under a transitional arrangement since 2022, conformant systems were due in late 2025, and there is **up to A$30 million in adoption grants**.
- In these systems, charting a dose is already a tap on a tablet.

**What this means for us:** "replaces manual charting" is weak. The real improvement is **what** gets charted. Today's record says "given"; the cup can add evidence that the dose was actually taken (chamber empty, water drunk, nothing left in the cup). To do that, the base must talk to the facility's eNRMC system. That's an integration job, not a hardware one.

## 5. Swallowing and crushing

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| Pooled prevalence of **dysphagia in RACFs: 56%** (range 16–70% across studies) | systematic review and meta-analysis, 2024 |
| **54%** of people in permanent residential aged care have **dementia** | AIHW |
| Over 90% of care workers meet residents with swallowing difficulties every day; over 94% modify medicines (such as crushing) | Australia-wide survey |
| Almost half of tablets crushed in memory support units | Qian 2016 |

**How crushing works:** the tablet is ground to powder, mixed into a teaspoon of soft food or thickened fluid, and given by spoon, usually one medicine at a time. It should be approved and recorded.

**Many tablets must not be crushed:**
- **Slow-release** (SR, XR, CR, modified release): crushing releases the whole dose at once.
- **Enteric-coated** tablets lose their protective coating.
- **Hazardous drugs** can harm the staff breathing in the powder.
- **Sublingual** (under-the-tongue) tablets don't work if swallowed.

Pharmacists check these against the reference *Don't Rush to Crush*.

**What this means for us:**

- Residents with **true dysphagia** are outside what the cup can do. So are residents who can't reliably lift a cup, or who hide or spit out tablets.
- There may be a second group: people with a **normal swallow who find tablets hard**, where crushing is partly for speed. Pill-swallowing cups (Oralflo and similar) exist for this group. If the group is big, "keeps slow-release and coated tablets whole" is a real selling point. **We don't know its size.**
- The guidelines require **swallow screening** before oral medicines are given. Any resident using the cup would need that screening first.

## 6. Self-administration: what the rules say

From the *Guiding Principles for Medication Management in Residential Aged Care Facilities* (Dept of Health, 2022), **Guiding Principle 14: Self-administration of medicines**:

- Residents have the **right to choose** to take their own medicines ("dignity of risk"). The facility must offer the option when a resident is assessed as capable and safe.
- This needs **consent** and a **formal assessment** by a doctor, nurse practitioner, pharmacist or registered nurse. The assessment is **reviewed at least every six months**, and whenever health or cognition changes.
- The facility's policies must cover **recording** self-administration, **monitoring adherence**, and reconciling blister packs against the medication chart.
- Self-administered medicines must be **kept away from other residents and visitors**.
- The recommended pack for self-administering residents is a **multi-dose blister or sachet**.
- Care workers may **assist** with self-administration.
- **Respite** residents should be supported to keep their medicine-taking skills for when they go home.

**Why this matters:** GP14 already allows residents to take their own medicines. What it asks the facility to add is locked storage, adherence monitoring and records. Those are exactly what the base and cup do. That makes the cup **a tool that makes self-administration safer and easier to audit**, and a legal route that already exists. It also means the cup **can't** be used by residents who haven't passed the assessment.

## 7. What already exists

| Tool | What it solves | What it doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| **Blister packs and sachet rolls** (Webster-pak, Portion-pak and similar); the pharmacy pre-sorts each dose time | **Sorting.** Already standard in Australian aged care | Reminders, on-time delivery, proof of swallowing |
| **Electronic medication charts** (eNRMC) | Prescribing, charting, alerts | Anything physical. It records what staff tap |
| **On-site pharmacists, medication reviews, regimen simplification** | Polypharmacy itself | Administration |
| **Hero** (US, home): holds a 90-day supply of 10 medicines, dispenses at dose times, alerts a caregiver if a dose is missed. From US$29.99/month | Home reminders, dispensing, missed-dose alerts | Knows pills were dispensed, not swallowed. Sorts from bulk bottles, so it duplicates the pharmacy's work. Not built for facilities |
| **Evondos** (Nordic, home care): takes **pharmacy sachet rolls** (up to a month per roll), gives spoken cues, alerts the home care nurse and locks the dose if it isn't taken. Pilot: 99% of doses retrieved | Home telecare dispensing that works with the pharmacy's packs | Knows the sachet was taken out, not swallowed |
| **AiCure** (clinical trials): phone camera and AI watch the patient swallow; over 97% accuracy reported | **Confirmed ingestion**, sold to drug trials | Needs a phone and a cooperative patient. Not for aged care |
| **Proteus / Abilify MyCite**: a sensor inside the pill | Confirmed ingestion | **A commercial failure**: Proteus went bankrupt in 2020. The pill cost over US$1,600 a month, and patients distrusted being tracked |
| **Oralflo and other pill-swallowing cups** | Helps people swallow tablets whole | No loading, cues, confirmation or records |

**Lessons:**

1. **Don't compete on sorting.** Pharmacies already do it. The base should take the pharmacy's sachets or pack as they arrive, as Evondos does. That makes spec §11 (sachet rolls) the lead option for the magazine.
2. **Nobody sells "confirmed swallow" in aged care.** That's the gap.
3. Proteus shows **confirmed ingestion alone doesn't sell** unless someone is paying for the proof. In a facility, the payer is the facility, and the benefit has to show up as staff time or fewer incidents.

## 8. Where the value is, and where it isn't

**Strong:**

- **Supported self-administration (GP14).** Residents who can take their own medicines get cues, doses on time, locked storage, and adherence monitoring and records done automatically. Respite residents keep their medicine-taking skills. The facility gets an audit trail GP14 already asks for.
- **Time-critical medicines** (Parkinson's first). Doses arrive on schedule, not when the round gets there. The catch: Parkinson's itself often causes **tremor and swallowing problems**, and late doses make both worse. Some of these residents won't be able to use the cup, especially later in the disease. Size this group with a facility, not from the 7.9% figure.
- **Evidence the dose was taken**, added to the eNRMC record, instead of just "given".
- **Hydration tracking** from the weighing, at no extra cost.

**Weak or absent:**

- **Polypharmacy itself.** That's the prescriber's and pharmacist's job.
- **Charting time.** Charting is already electronic.
- **Residents with dysphagia, advanced dementia, or who hide or spit out tablets.** These are the residents who take the most nurse time.

**The core tension:** the cup helps the residents who are **easiest** to give medicines to, not the hardest. The nurse time saved per resident is therefore modest, and the value comes from **timing, proof and independence** rather than big labour savings.

**Back-of-envelope, with every number an assumption to check with a facility:**

- Suppose the cup lets staff skip the wait-and-watch for an eligible resident: **1–2 minutes** per round, 4 rounds a day. That's **4–8 minutes a day**, or roughly **25–50 staff hours a year** per resident.
- At an assumed loaded wage of A$45/hour, that's **about A$1,100–2,200 a year** per resident, against a device budget of about US$400. It pays back **only if staff really do step away**, which GP14 permits only for assessed residents.
- If **under ~15% of a facility's residents** qualify, most of the labour case disappears and the pitch rests on timing, proof and independence.

## 9. The original proposal's claims, checked

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "Nearly half of adults 65+ are affected by polypharmacy" | Not checked here. Find the source before reusing it |
| Confusion is a distinct cause of non-adherence | Plausible **at home**. In a facility, nurses manage the regimen, so the confusion sits with staff |
| Cuts charting time by 40–50% | **Not supported.** Charting is electronic (eNRMC) |
| Cuts confusion-related missed or incorrect doses by 20% | No evidence yet. Missed doses are real (§3), but the figure is a guess |
| "Residents gain greater independence" | **Supported**, via the GP14 self-administration pathway (§6) |
| Tablets swallowed whole, no crushing | Supported as a goal. Excludes residents with dysphagia (§5) |
| "A gap unaddressed by consumer dispensers" | **Mostly true.** Home dispensers alert on missed doses; none confirms the swallow, and none is built for facilities |
| Release gate and sip sensor in the cup | **Superseded** by v2 (DESIGN_NOTES §5, CONCEPTS) |

## 10. Questions only a facility or speech pathologist can answer

**Facility (a nurse and a manager):**

1. Where does the time in a round actually go? How much is waiting for the swallow?
2. What share of your residents swallow whole tablets with thin water and can lift a cup?
3. How many are on the self-administration pathway now? How many could be with support?
4. Would you let a GP14-assessed resident take doses from a locked, logging device without staff present?
5. How are time-critical medicines (Parkinson's) handled today? How often are they late?
6. Which eNRMC do you use, and would you want dose evidence written into it?

**Speech pathologist:**

1. Is 1–3 whole tablets with the first mouthful of thin water acceptable for residents with no dysphagia?
2. Is a short, wide spout lid acceptable, or does it encourage head extension?
3. How big is the group with a normal swallow who just find tablets hard, compared with true dysphagia?

**Stop and rethink if:** fewer than ~15% of residents qualify, facilities won't reduce supervision for assessed residents, or the speech pathologist says tablets with thin water are unsafe even without dysphagia. In any of those cases the facility pitch is weak, and the **home market** is the place to look instead: there nobody watches, and proof the dose was taken matters most.
